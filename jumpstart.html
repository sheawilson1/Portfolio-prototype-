The read

Clients don’t want a dashboard. They want to know someone good is watching their money. So every JumpStart report answers four questions: what changed, why it matters, what the agency is doing next, and whether you need to decide anything.

What it is

A reporting product for Influential’s paid media clients and the team that runs their accounts. Clients get a briefing in plain English. The agency runs every account from one desk, and any report can go out as a deck, a PDF, an A4 report or an email.

What I did

Everything from the audit on: research, product thinking, interface, tone of voice and the build. I designed in the browser and built with Claude Code and Codex, so James and Alex were always reviewing something they could click.

Where it is now

The National Football Museum pilot ran on live Meta data behind a client password until the campaign ended on 31 August. The agency desk is built behind a work sign-in and still runs on sample accounts. A public site is on the way.

9 June

A rough prototype and a good instinct

James handed me a prototype and a name on 9 June. His pitch was paid media reporting with a friendlier front door. The tools agencies use felt clunky and heavy on data, and he wanted something closer to Monzo or Slack.

My audit said the instinct was right and the product was still a concept. The AI summary had nobody behind it. The client’s question box went nowhere. The login was a role picker. A second brief, drafted with AI, described client onboarding instead of reporting, so I went with what James had said in the room and made reporting the first job.

The insight that became the brief

The email was the product.

Every month an account lead would read the platform numbers, work out what happened and rewrite it as an email the client could use. The email was always better than the dashboard, because it had a verdict, a reason and a next step.

June

Everyone sells the data. Nobody writes the read.

I went through the tools agencies already pay for, including Funnel, Supermetrics, AgencyAnalytics, Whatagraph, DashThis, Databox and Looker Studio. They’re strong on connectors, templates and scheduled reports. Every one of them assumes the reader already knows what CPA means.

The person reading a JumpStart report doesn’t. She’s a marketing lead with a budget to defend and a finance director who asks every month whether the ads are working. The night before that meeting she feels a small, familiar dread, because she can’t tell.

10 to 24 June

Good-looking, but soulless

The early versions came quickly. By 20 June there was a working build with real sign-in and separate client views, plus a line of static prototypes I could change in minutes. Each version added what the team asked for: sign-off, a cheat sheet, benchmarks, a funnel, a channel comparison.

Then I looked at V3 and felt it was good-looking but soulless. That happens when you build before discovery has finished. So I went back to the start and ran the process properly. I set up three AI agents with one job each: a researcher for the client’s real worries, a designer for tone and feeling, and an editor for what to cut. Then I pulled what they found into one direction.

Six principles came out of it, and they still hold.

  1. Verdict before evidence

    Every screen opens with a plain sentence, then the numbers that prove it.

  2. Show the watching

    “We noticed Tuesday, we acted Wednesday” does more for trust than a green number.

  3. Calm scales with stakes

    The more money at risk, the plainer the page. A bad number is never cute.

  4. Soul is a gradient

    Personality lives at the top of the page and fades to nothing in the tables.

  5. Features earn depth, not height

    The first screen is a fixed length. New things go behind a click.

  6. One colour, one job

    Each colour means one thing, and nothing else borrows it. Red is for broken.

Where the personality lives

The read

Warm

A greeting, the verdict in one sentence, numbers that count up. This is where the voice lives.

The evidence

Neutral

The trend, the comparison, what we’re doing next. Human, but tighter. Colour carries meaning here.

The detail

Silent

Campaign tables. A fast, quiet, normal dashboard. A finance lead should never have to fight the personality to read a figure.

25 June

The version the team signed off

V5 brought it together. The read sat at the top, a “needs you today” list held anything waiting for sign-off, client questions reached a real person, and the report exported to A4. James and Alex reviewed it on 25 June.

What the review changed

  • Benchmarks. Compare against the industry, not our own client list, which could make us look bad against the wrong sector.
  • Colour. Red only for things that are broken, like a failed sync. Green and amber follow strict rules.
  • Rings. The circular performance rings went. They looked like progress without saying anything.
  • Kept. The onboarding flow, the sign-off section and the conversational language.

They also wanted help justifying budgets in first meetings. I built a planner that works both ways. Start from the spend, or start from the goal and work back to the spend it needs. V5 became the approved reference at jump-start.app/approved, and later the first version to run on a real client’s data.

July

I stopped generating looks

July was for visual direction. I tried a white editorial version, warm paper, a navy and orange brand, a lime bolt, a museum painting behind the numbers and a pixel pattern. Some were beautiful. None of them fixed the real problem, which was the order things appear in.

On 11 July I stopped and went back to first principles. A report should make a claim, show the evidence, then let you inspect it. Most dashboards do it the other way round and leave the client to find the story.

The same week, said two ways

A dashboard saysCPA increased 18% week on week and CTR decreased 7% across Meta prospecting campaigns.

JumpStart saysProspecting got more expensive this week. Weaker click quality on Meta is the cause, so we are cutting the lowest-performing ad set and testing new creative before touching the budget.

A dashboard saysMeta API sync failed.

JumpStart saysMeta data is delayed, so this week’s view is incomplete. Google and Shopify are up to date. We will refresh the Meta figures before this report is shared.

A dashboard saysAll KPIs within expected parameters. No anomalies detected.

JumpStart saysNo action needed. Everything is on track and we are leaving the account alone this week.

“No action needed” became one of the most important states in the product. A short report is proof that someone looked.

21 July to 13 August

Real numbers change the design

On 21 July I connected the National Football Museum’s Meta account to V5. A real campaign in the approved design taught me more than any mockup had. It also showed what we couldn’t honestly say yet. I took out the demo targets, benchmarks, funnel claims and invented action plans, and kept only what Meta reported.

Rules for numbers a client can trust

  • Scope by campaign ID, never by name, because names change.
  • Use the account’s own attribution windows, so totals match Ads Manager.
  • Map each purchase label by hand, so nothing is counted twice.
  • Count link clicks, not every click, and never add reach across platforms.
  • Say when a number isn’t available. Never guess it.

On 13 August the client page went behind a password at jump-start.app/nfm. It got its own release branch, so my design work couldn’t change what the client saw by accident.

20 to 26 August

A budget holder doesn’t want a dashboard

In late August the report became a briefing. It opens with one sentence, “Every £1 you spent brought back £2.86”, says whether anything needs you, and the account lead checks it before it reaches you. The numbers are there to back up the sentence.

Alex said it still looked complicated next to the Monzo and Slack feel James wanted. My first move was to cut, and I took out the week on week section. That was wrong. The page got emptier and no clearer. I put it back and redesigned it with paired bars, a plain line under each and changes written out, like “Down 8%, getting cheaper”.

Then I worked on the tone. The briefing was correct but cold, so I built the same report at four temperatures, from plain to handwritten, and judged each one against the last.

  1. 1Emoji as seasoning

  2. 2A human voice Shipped

  3. 3Celebration motion

  4. 4Annotated by hand

The second one shipped, with the counter from the third. Most of the warmth lived in the words: a greeting, a verdict, a sign-off. These are the rules I kept.

  • Personality goes on the good news itself, never on buttons or chrome.
  • The page goes quiet when something needs a decision.
  • Confetti, badges and a musical sound all got cut. They read as the product celebrating itself.
  • Numbers roll in like a counter, one at a time, left to right.

27 August to 4 September

One desk for every account

The agency needed its own view. The desk opens with what needs a person: broken things first, then decisions, then reports waiting to be signed. “1 connection down, and 4 other things for a person.” Anything that’s fine stays out of the way.

Two details show how I work. I tried eight sounds for the desk and kept three. The three I kept had measurable things in common: quiet peaks, a low body under the click, nothing high for long. So I wrote those down as rules, and a test now checks every new sound against them.

The logo lockup got the same treatment. A mark next to the JumpStart wordmark is aligned from two numbers measured off its own file, instead of being nudged until it looks right.

September

Every report goes out the way the client likes it

Clients forward reports to their boss, so the export matters as much as the page. One dialog serves the client and the agency. Pick any two dates, see the deck before it goes, then send it as a PowerPoint deck, a PDF of the deck, a full A4 report or an email. It remembers each client’s style: House, Night or Dawn. Until a person signs a report off, JumpStart stamps it as a draft.

First use got the same care. The agency sets a client up in seven steps. The client gets a short welcome that explains the briefing before they see their numbers.

What I’d tell myself in June

Order beats cutting

When a page looks complicated, the fix is usually the order and the wording. Taking things away just made it emptier.

Real data is a design tool

The first live campaign removed more bad ideas than any review did.

Write taste down

Principles, sound rules and measured lockups meant the next version argued from a rule instead of from adjectives.

Choosing is the job

With AI I could build a whole direction in an afternoon and throw it away. Knowing which one was right was still on me.

What’s next

JumpStart is still in development, so these screens will change. Next, it keeps a record of every decision: what JumpStart suggested, how the account lead changed it, what the client said and what happened after. The ad platforms don’t have that, and it’s what will make the suggestions better. The name might change too.
